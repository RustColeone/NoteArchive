gm/ID notes: characterize once, size by lookup

How to measure your process once, turn it into four charts, and then size any transistor by reading numbers off them instead of guessing and re-simulating.

Every curve on this page is real simulation data from our 1.1 V core devices. Follow the steps in order the first time; after that, jump straight to steps 7 and 8.

Step 0

Why bother: the one-number idea

The old textbook way sizes a transistor from the square law, ID = ½·μCox·(W/L)·Vov2. In a 40 nm process that equation is wrong by a lot, so you end up sizing, simulating, re-sizing, and simulating again.

The gm/ID method replaces the equation with a measurement. You pick one number for each transistor, gm/ID, and that single choice fixes almost everything else about it:

  • how much current each micron of width carries,
  • how much gain it can give,
  • how fast it can be,
  • how much voltage headroom it eats.

You read those off charts you made once from the real models, so the answer is right the first time.

gm/ID is "how much gm you get for each µA you spend." High gm/ID (around 20) is efficient but slow and needs a wide device. Low gm/ID (around 5) is fast and small but burns current. Most analog transistors live between 8 and 18.

Step 1

Words you need

gm (transconductance)
How much the drain current changes when you wiggle the gate voltage. Units: siemens (A/V). This is what makes an amplifier amplify.
ID
The DC drain current you bias the transistor with. This is what costs power.
gm/ID
gm divided by ID. Units: 1/V. Efficiency: gm per amp of current spent.
ID/W (current density)
Current per micron of width, in µA/µm. Once you know ID and this, the width is simply W = ID ÷ (ID/W).
ro and gm·ro (intrinsic gain)
ro is the transistor's own output resistance: how little the drain current changes when the drain voltage moves (the simulator reports its inverse, gds = 1/ro). gm·ro is the most voltage gain one transistor can give by itself.
Cgg and fT (transit frequency)
Cgg is the total capacitance looking into the gate. fT = gm / (2π·Cgg) is the frequency where the transistor stops giving any current gain. A rough speed limit.
Vdsat
The minimum drain-source voltage that keeps the transistor in saturation. Smaller means more headroom for the other transistors in the stack.
L (channel length)
The second knob. Longer L gives more gain but is slower. Short L is fast but has little gain.
Step 2

The test bench: one NMOS, one PMOS

We measure a single 1 µm wide transistor of each type. Its drain is held at half the supply, the gate is swept from off to fully on, and we repeat that sweep for several channel lengths.

  1. Width fixed at 1 µm. Then current in µA is current per µm, with no extra division.
  2. L is a parameter, not separate instances. One transistor, swept over 40n, 60n, 100n, 200n, 500n and 1u.
  3. Drain at VDD/2 = 0.55 V by its own voltage source. That's a typical bias in a real circuit.
  4. Source and bulk tied together (to ground for NMOS, to VDD for PMOS), so there is no body effect.
  5. Gate swept 0 to 1.1 V in 5 mV steps. Both devices see |VGS| equal to the sweep value.

We're filling in a big spreadsheet: for every gate voltage and every length, what are the current, gm, ro (gds) and Cgg? Everything later is just reading that spreadsheet.

Step 4

Run it and check it isn't nonsense

afs tran.sp -o out/lut        # or your team's run script
ezwave out/lut.sw0 &          # open the waveforms

In EZwave, the DC folder holds the computed signals (GMIDN, IDWN, ...). Each one has 6 sub-curves, one per L. Before trusting anything, do these three checks:

  1. gm/ID at low gate voltage flattens out at about 25 to 35. Physics caps it at 1/(n·kT/q) ≈ 38/n, where n is 1.1 to 1.5. Ours: 25 (40n) up to 31 (1u) for NMOS. ✔
  2. gm/ID at full gate drive drops to about 2 to 3. Ours: about 2.1. ✔
  3. The log file says "0 errors". Warnings about ignored options are fine. A warning that says Device ... not found means a probe name is wrong.

.print can't use .probe names. .print dc gmidn gives "Device not found". The binary file already has everything, so skip .print.

Step 5

The four charts and what each one is for

The trick: plot everything against gm/ID, not against gate voltage. Gate voltage depends on threshold, which moves with L, corner and temperature. gm/ID doesn't care, so these curves stay valid when the threshold shifts.

Moving right on any chart (higher gm/ID) means more efficient, wider, slower. Moving down the L list (longer) means more gain and slower. Every design choice is a trade between those two knobs.

Step 7

From specs to gm/ID: the design path, in order

You can't pick any gm/ID you like, and you don't pick it first. For the input pair (M1/M2) of a two-stage Miller op-amp, the specs are used in a fixed order: GBW gives gm, slew rate gives ID, and their ratio is gm/ID. Gain then picks L, and speed and headroom check the result. Change any spec in the panel below and watch each step move.

The intuition first: the first stage is a current source charging Cc

In a Miller op-amp the second stage holds node X almost still, so whatever current the input pair puts out has nowhere to go except into Cc. The output voltage is just the voltage building up on that capacitor. Your I·t = C·V picture, I = C·dV/dt, is exactly right, and both specs come out of it:

Small input → GBW
i = gm1·vin  ⇒  vout = i / (jωCc)  ⇒  |A| = gm1 / (ωCc)
Say it out loud: "a sine wave at the input makes the pair pump current in and out of Cc. The faster the sine, the less time each half-cycle has to charge the cap, so the output swing shrinks as 1/f." Gain hits 1 when one half-cycle only charges Cc to the same amplitude as the input: ω = gm1/Cc, so gm1 = 2π·GBW·Cc. More gm pumps harder; a bigger cap needs more pumping for the same swing.
Big input → slew rate
imax = Itail = 2ID1  ⇒  SR = dVout/dt = Itail/Cc
Same picture, but the pump runs out. For a big step, one side of the pair takes the whole tail current and the other shuts off. The cap can't be charged faster than Itail, so the output ramps at a fixed V/µs no matter how big the step is. Slew is the large-signal limit of the same charging; GBW is its small-signal speed.

Divide one by the other and the capacitor cancels: SR / (2π·GBW) = Itail / gm1 = 2 / (gm/ID). So gm/ID is literally "how hard the pair pumps per volt of input" versus "how much current it has in total". A high gm/ID pair is efficient at small signals but runs out of current at a smaller input, so it slews sooner. Roughly, the pair saturates once vin exceeds about 2/(gm/ID), which is its Vov.

The path, step by step

① GBW → gm₁
gm1 = 2π · GBW · Cc
Cc is chosen first, from stability: about 0.2 to 0.5 × CL for 60° phase margin. GBW fixes gm only, not the current.
② Slew rate → minimum ID
Itail ≥ SR · Cc  ⇒  ID1 ≥ SR · Cc / 2
This is the step that gives you a current. Anything less and the cap can't be charged fast enough.
③ Their ratio → gm/ID
gm/ID = gm1 / ID1 ≤ 2π·GBW·Cc / (SR·Cc/2) = 4π·GBW / SR
Using exactly the slew-rate current gives the highest gm/ID you can use. Spending more current (to gain headroom or speed) moves you to a lower gm/ID.
④ DC gain → pick L
A1 = gm1·(ro2 ∥ ro4) ≈ ½·gmro  ⇒  need gmro ≥ 2·A1
This step needs no current. gmro = (gm/ID) × (ID·ro), and ID·ro is the device's Early voltage, set by L and bias, not by ID. Double ID with double W: gm doubles, ro halves, gain stays. So read the gm·ro chart at the gm/ID from step ③, and pick the shortest L that clears 2·A1.
⑤ Speed check
fT(gm/ID, L) ≥ k · GBW  (k ≈ 10)
Longer L is slower, so step ④ can break this. The device's own capacitance must not add poles near GBW.
⑥ Headroom check
Vov ≈ 2/(gm/ID) ≤ Vov,max  ⇒  gm/ID ≥ 2/Vov,max
From the step-10 headroom budget (VCM range, swing). If gm/ID from step ③ is too low here, you can't fix it by spending current: relax the slew spec or the headroom.
⑦ Width
W = ID1 / (ID/W at that gm/ID and L)
Only now does the ID/W chart come in. The whole window maps to a band of allowed ID/W, and so a range of widths.

Try it

gain limit (min) headroom limit (min) speed limit (max) slew limit (max) allowed window
① Gain chart: the horizontal line is the gm·ro you need. The curve must sit above it.
② fT chart: the horizontal line is the speed you need. The curve must sit above it.
③ ID/W chart: the window's edges map to a band of allowed current densities.

If the window is empty, two specs are fighting. The usual fixes: a longer L (raises the gain curve, so the gain line moves left), a smaller GBW·k or a lower SR spec, or more headroom. With the defaults, try L = 100 nm and you'll see gain and slew collide; L = 200 nm opens the window.

How it got there
    Step 9

    Sizing the whole circuit: every transistor by its job

    The calculator sizes one transistor. A real circuit has many, but you size every one of them with the same five questions. The only thing that changes from device to device is which spec it serves, and that decides its gm/ID and L.

    1. What is its job? Amplify (input pair, gain stage), carry current (tail, load, current source), shield (cascode), or copy a bias (diode, mirror reference).
    2. What current does it carry? This comes from the topology, not the charts. The tail carries 2×ID₁, each half of the pair carries ID₁, and the load carries the same ID₁.
    3. Which spec does it serve? That picks gm/ID. Amplifiers want gm (high gm/ID). Current sources want low gm (low gm/ID) and matching. Cascodes want little headroom (high gm/ID).
    4. Pick L. Long for gain, matching and output resistance; short for speed.
    5. Width falls out: W = ID ÷ (ID/W), read off the chart at that gm/ID and L. Then check the headroom budget (below).

    Input pair (M1, M2)gm₁ / (gm/ID)gm per µA, noise, offset; capped by slew and speed (step 7)12–18from gain (step 7)
    Mirror load / current-source load (M3, M4)same as the pairkeep gm small: its noise and offset are scaled by gmload/gm₁6–10long (≥ 2–5× Lmin): ro and matching

    Amplifying devices want high gm/ID because gm is the product. Current-carrying devices want low gm/ID because for them gm is a nuisance: it turns their own threshold mismatch and noise into output current.

    Mirrors and "why m = 6": unit cells

    In a mirror, never size the copies by making W different. Make one unit transistor and place it m times. The ratio is then exactly m, because edge effects, stress and rounding scale with it too. The recipe:

    1. Choose the reference current Iref. In a real chip, the bias block hands out a standard current, typically 5 to 20 µA. Smaller saves power but is noisier and more sensitive to leakage.
    2. m = Iout ÷ Iref, rounded to an integer. Then nudge Iref so the product is exact. Keep m between about 1 and 10: a 1:57 mirror multiplies the reference's noise and error by 57, and its input pole (gmref/C of 58 gates) gets slow.
    3. Size the unit cell like a current source: gm/ID ≈ 8, long L. Its ID/W gives the unit width.
    Our tail, worked
    Itail = 57 µA, Iref ≈ 10 µA ⇒ m = 5.7 → 6, Iref = 57/6 = 9.5 µA
    unit: NMOS, L = 1 µm, gm/ID = 8 ⇒ ID/W = 9.12 µA/µm ⇒ W = 9.5 / 9.12 ≈ 1.04 µm
    Reference = 1 unit (1 µm / 1 µm), tail = 6 units. Vdsat ≈ 2/8 = 0.25 V (model: 0.20 V), so the input common mode needs ≥ VGS₁ + 0.25 V.
    The PMOS mirror load, worked
    ID = 28.5 µA, PMOS, L = 500 nm, gm/ID = 8 ⇒ ID/W = 5.35 µA/µm ⇒ W ≈ 5.3 µm
    Both load devices are identical (a 1:1 mirror). Not 10 µm: that copies the input pair's gm target, which the load doesn't have.

    Cascodes: sizing the device on top

    A cascode doesn't need gain or matching. It only has to be "good enough" in two ways, and use as little headroom as possible:

    • its gm·ro multiplies the output resistance: Rout ≈ (gmro)c · ro,main;
    • its source node has a pole at about gm,c/Cnode, which must sit well above GBW.

    Both are easy, so give it a high gm/ID (small Vdsat) and a moderate L. It carries the same current as the device under it.

    Example: cascoding the 57 µA tail
    main (bottom): gm/ID = 8, L = 1 µm ⇒ gm = 456 µS, gm·ro = 39 ⇒ ro ≈ 86 kΩ
    cascode (top): gm/ID = 15, L = 200 nm ⇒ ID/W = 7.17 µA/µm ⇒ W ≈ 8 µm; gm·ro ≈ 40
    Rout ≈ 40 × 86 kΩ ≈ 3.4 MΩ (vs 86 kΩ uncascoded)  ·  headroom ≈ 2/8 + 2/15 ≈ 0.38 V
    Cascode gate bias: Vb = VGSc + Vdsatmain + ~50 mV of margin, so the bottom device stays saturated.

    The check that ties it together: the headroom budget

    Every gm/ID choice spends voltage (Vdsat ≈ 2/(gm/ID)). Add up each stack from rail to rail and make sure it fits in 1.1 V with the signal swing left over:

    VCM,min = VGS₁ + Vdsat₅   ·   VCM,max = VDD − VSG₃ + VTH₁   ·   Vout,stage1 ≈ VDD − VSG₄

    If a stack doesn't fit, raise gm/ID on the device with the most slack (usually a cascode or the tail). If that breaks matching or speed, the topology has run out of room. That's the real reason designs drop cascodes at low supply or move to folded structures.

    Step 10

    Worked example: a real first stage, from charts to simulation

    This is an actual run: a 5-transistor first stage (NMOS pair, PMOS mirror load, NMOS tail), sized with this page, netlisted from Cadence and simulated with ALPS. We'll go through what was planned, what was built, what the simulator said, and the one thing that went wrong, which is the most useful part.

    1 · The plan (from the charts)

    1. gm from the spec. GBW = 50 MHz into C = 1 pF ⇒ gm₁ = 2π × 50 MHz × 1 pF = 314 µS.
    2. L from gain. L = 500 nm gives gm·ro ≈ 40 at gm/ID ≈ 11, so the stage gain should be about half of that, around 20.
    3. gm/ID = 11 (inside the step-7 window), so ID = 314 µS ÷ 11 = 28.5 µA per side and the tail is 57 µA.
    4. Width: ID/W at gm/ID 11, L 500n = 8.36 µA/µm ⇒ W = 28.5 ÷ 8.36 = 3.4 µm, built as 3.5 µm.
    5. Tail: 10 µA reference (1 µm / 500 nm diode), tail = m = 6 copies ⇒ 60 µA, so 30 µA per side.

    4 · The AC result, and the surprise

    Gain is 28 dB (×25) and GBW is 28.8 MHz, not the planned 50. The hand model built from the .op0 numbers (dashed) lands right on the simulation, so the circuit is behaving exactly as its bias says. The bias is what's wrong. The operating-point file shows why:

    Cause: the input common mode. The sources had no DC value, so the simulator defaulted them to 0.55 V. The pair needs VGS ≈ 0.50 V, which leaves only 0.55 − 0.50 = 0.05 V across the tail. That's far below its Vdsat of 0.15 V, so the tail falls into the linear region, behaves like a resistor, and passes less than half its current.

    The headroom budget that was violated
    VCM,min = VGS₁ + Vdsat,tail ≈ 0.55 + 0.15 + body effect ≈ 0.72 V
    VGS₁ is read at the planned 30 µA (gm/ID ≈ 10.9 ⇒ 0.553 V), not the starved value. Use VCM = 0.75 V: v1 vip vss DC=0.75 AC 0.5 and v4 vin vss DC=0.75 AC -0.5, so the differential input is 1 V and the plot reads gain directly.

    With the tail back in saturation, the charts predict gm₁ ≈ 327 µS, a stage gain of about ×22 (27 dB) and GBW ≈ 50 MHz if the tail delivers its full 60 µA: the dotted curve. Part 6 shows what really happened. Gain drops slightly because the pair runs at a lower gm/ID (10.9 instead of 14.5). That's the trade the window in step 7 describes.

    The simulation "just worked" and still missed the spec by 43%. The charts caught it, because they told us what gm to expect, so a 28.8 MHz result was obviously wrong rather than "close enough". Always check the operating-point file for any device in linear before reading AC results.

    5 · The transient run

    Waveform not shown. The transient was saved as .fsdb, which this page can't read. Re-run with .option post=1 (HSPICE .tr0) to get it plotted here in the page's colors.

    The inputs swing ±0.55 V around 0.55 V, a 2.2 V differential swing into a gain of 25, so the output just slams between the rails. That's expected for an open-loop stage. Transient tests of an amplifier belong in closed loop (for example an inverting gain of −1), or with millivolt-level open-loop inputs.

    Also note the high-frequency phase turning back up (about −80° to −65°) above 20 MHz. That's the zero from the 1 kΩ resistor in series with the 1 pF load (1/2πRC ≈ 160 MHz), not the amplifier.

    The circuit at its operating point (run 2)

    GBW is now within 2% of the charts. Two smaller gaps remain, and both are textbook effects worth recognizing:

    1. The tail gives 50.7 µA, not 60. The reference diode sits at VDS = 0.567 V but the tail sits at only 0.173 V, just 25 mV above its Vdsat. A mirror only copies exactly when both sides have the same VDS. The charts give 1/(ID·ro) = (gm/ID)/(gm·ro) = 10.2/38 ≈ 0.27 /V, so a 0.39 V VDS difference costs about 11%, and sitting at the saturation edge costs the rest. Fixes: a higher VCM, a cascoded tail, or a reference whose VDS matches the tail's.
    2. Gain is ×19.6, not ×22.9. The input transistors see VDS = 0.546 − 0.173 = 0.37 V, not the bench's 0.55 V, and ro drops at low VDS. This is the "my circuit's VDS isn't 0.55" question in step 11: for gain-critical devices, rerun the bench at the real VDS.

    Every gap between the charts and the simulator traced back to a bias condition: VCM, VDS or VSB. None of them came from the method itself. So the habit is: predict, simulate, compare, and when they disagree, read the operating point.

    7 · Headroom, worked from the charts

    Headroom is just bookkeeping: walk every path from VDD to ground. Each gate-source drop is a VGS, read off the gm/ID-vs-VGS chart at that device's current density. Each device that must stay saturated needs VDS ≥ Vdsat + ~50 mV, with Vdsat ≈ 2/(gm/ID) as the safe estimate.

    Where X = 0.546 V comes from
    M3 is a diode: VX = VDD − VSG₃
    M3: ID = 30 µA (planned), W = 10 µm ⇒ ID/W = 3.0 µA/µm ⇒ PMOS chart, L = 500n: gm/ID ≈ 10.5, VSG ≈ 0.559 V
    VX ≈ 1.1 − 0.559 = 0.541 V  (simulated: 0.546 V; at the real 25.3 µA the chart gives 0.556 V)
    Because M4 copies M3, the output sits at the same voltage when the inputs are balanced, so Vout ≈ X too.

    8 · Fixing the tail: the wide-swing cascode mirror

    The tail lost 15% of its current because M8 (a diode) sits at VDS = VGS ≈ 0.57 V while M5 sits at 0.17 V. A cascode mirror forces both bottom devices to the same small VDS, so the copy is exact and the output resistance goes up about 40×. The "wide-swing" version keeps that VDS just above Vdsat instead of a full VGS, and this self-biased form needs only one resistor:

    1. Bottom devices (M8, M5): current sources, so gm/ID = 10 at L = 1 µm ⇒ ID/W = 5.5 µA/µm ⇒ unit W = 1.8 µm, M5 = 6 units. Target VDS = Vdsat + margin ≈ 0.20 V.
    2. Cascodes (Mc8, Mc5): high gm/ID = 15 at L = 200 nm for small Vdsat ⇒ ID/W = 7.2 µA/µm ⇒ unit W = 1.4 µm. VGS ≈ 0.51 V plus about 45 mV of body effect, because their source sits at 0.2 V.
    3. R sets the gap between the two gate lines: VA − VB = Iref·R must equal VC + VGSc − VGS₈ ≈ 0.20 + 0.555 − 0.555 = 0.20 V ⇒ R = 20 kΩ.
    4. Cost: the tail node now needs VC + Vdsatc ≈ 0.33 V instead of 0.15 V, so VCM,min rises to about 0.93 V. With VCM,max ≈ 1.0 V, the input range shrinks to about 70 mV.

    The cascode fixes the current error completely, but at 1.1 V it nearly closes the input window. That's why the next step is a replica bias: copy the tail's VDS into the reference branch without stacking another device under the pair. Same accuracy, no headroom cost.

    Step 11

    Questions people ask

    How do we get fT without running an AC simulation?

    At every DC point the simulator already computes the small-signal values: gm, gds and the capacitances. An AC simulation uses those exact numbers. fT is where the current gain gm/(jω·Cgg) falls to 1, so fT = gm/(2π·Cgg) comes straight from the DC data.

    This is the intrinsic fT, without layout parasitics, so the real device is a bit slower. To prove it, put an AC source on the gate with the drain shorted to AC ground, and find where |idrain/igate| = 1.

    Why plot against gm/ID and not overdrive voltage (VGS − VT)?

    Look at the gm/ID vs VGS chart: the short-L curves cross the long-L ones. Short devices here have a higher threshold (the reverse short-channel effect from halo implants), so the same VGS means a different operating point at each L. gm/ID describes the operating point directly, whatever the threshold does.

    In the square law, gm/ID = 2/Vov. That's a handy way to translate: gm/ID = 10 roughly means Vov ≈ 0.2 V.

    Why is Vdsat smaller than 2/(gm/ID)?

    The square law says Vdsat = Vov = 2/(gm/ID). Real short-channel devices saturate earlier because of velocity saturation, and the BSIM model reports that smaller value. For headroom budgets, the conservative choice is to use 2/(gm/ID) plus about 50 mV.

    Why is the gain at L = 40n so low (around 9)?

    At short L the drain voltage reaches into the channel (DIBL and channel-length modulation), so ro is small. That's why analog gain devices are drawn at several times the minimum length.

    My circuit's transistor has VDS = 0.2 V, not 0.55. Are the charts still right?

    ID/W and fT shift only a few percent. Gain (gm·ro) depends more on VDS, so for a cascode or a low-VDS device, rerun the bench at that VDS. The same goes for a source not at the bulk voltage (VSB ≠ 0): rerun with that VSB.

    What about other corners and temperatures?

    Rerun the same bench with the other corner file (SS, FF) and .temp. The nice property of gm/ID is that the ID/W-vs-gm/ID curves move much less across corners than the VGS-based ones, so a design sized at TT usually holds up.
